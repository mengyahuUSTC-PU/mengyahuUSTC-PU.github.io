### [Woman says her stepfather used Grok to turn her childhood photo into 7,000 abuse images](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/08/15/woman-alleges-grok-made-thousands-sexual-abuse-images-childhood-snap/)

The Washington Post reported that a Wyoming woman, named as Jane Doe 4 in court filings, has joined the federal lawsuit three Tennessee teenagers brought against xAI (now part of SpaceX); the suit is seeking class action status. Per the complaint: her stepfather uploaded a photo of her at age 11 lying on a couch, used Grok to generate more than 7,000 sexual abuse images of her, and traded them with others on social platforms. He died by suicide two days after a law enforcement search. Two details in the complaint deserve a close read. First, why he picked Grok: the platform "was less restrictive than other AI models" and acted on prompts to sexualize an image of a prepubescent child. Second, xAI did file a tip in February with NCMEC (the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, the US clearinghouse platforms are legally required to notify about suspected child sexual abuse material, which then routes cases to law enforcement), but the tip allegedly included only the original photo, and xAI did not respond when investigators asked for the Grok-generated images and IP records. An NCMEC report is how these cases enter the legal system; what evidence it carries and whether the platform cooperates afterward decide whether a case can move at all. That is the link the complaint says broke. These are one side's allegations, and xAI has not responded to requests for comment (see also [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/15/woman-claims-her-stepfather-used-grok-to-transform-childhood-photo-into-explicit-imagery/)).

### [Cursor is now part of SpaceX](https://cursor.com/blog/joining-spacex)

Cursor (maker Anysphere) announced on its blog that the SpaceX acquisition has closed: a $60 billion all-stock deal that began in April as a partnership with a purchase option, was signed on June 16, and became effective August 14 according to a securities filing. Cursor joins the SpaceXAI team, says it now has access to "the largest fleet of GPUs in the world," and lists helping "make Grok the world's most useful AI" among its goals, alongside continued work on the Grok API and Cursor itself (see also [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/15/spacex-officially-closes-its-cursor-acquisition/)). My view: Cursor's selling point has been model neutrality, switching freely between Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google models. Its owner now builds a competing model and put serving Grok in the closing announcement. Enterprise customers should ask two concrete questions: whether model routing inside the product will start favoring the house model, and where their code goes under the new corporate structure. One more connection: xAI, the defendant in the story above, sits under the same SpaceX roof, so the model, the coding tool, and the litigation now share one owner.

### [Derek Lowe asks how AI drug discovery is doing, really](https://www.science.org/content/blog-post/so-how-ai-drug-discovery-doing-really)

Derek Lowe, a veteran medicinal chemist, took stock of AI drug discovery in his In the Pipeline column on Science's site. His conclusion: AI methods have been developed, applied, and benchmarked at scale in this field, yet evidence of clinically relevant impact remains "disappointingly limited." The mechanism behind his skepticism is worth restating. Most drug failures happen in human trials, on efficacy or safety; what AI currently speeds up is early discovery, whose time and cost are a rounding error next to clinical trials. So the real test of whether AI has changed the industry is Phase II success rates (the first stage where a drug's efficacy is tested in patients), and that evidence has not appeared. Worth reading if you work on AI for science: it separates "how many AI-designed molecules are in the pipeline" from "has the industry's success rate moved," and only the second is the output metric.
