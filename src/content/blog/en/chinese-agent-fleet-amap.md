At 20:57 UTC on September 28, twenty scan reports appeared on urlquery.net, a free public URL-scanning service, all pointed at the same target: the Amap (Gaode Maps) page for Beijing's Summer Palace. Over the next week a group of independent researchers watched the number snowball: 2,048 reports covering 216 places in China, parks, zoos, hospitals, temples, with 1,810 reports on October 4 alone. They published a [preliminary report](https://swarmcha.se/posts/chinese-agent-fleet) that day and updated it the next: a fleet of AI agents running on Tencent Cloud, with evidence pointing to Tencent's Hunyuan models, was systematically reading one obscure kind of Amap data, namely which entrance visitors to each place actually use.

Take Chengdu Zoo: north gate 71%, east gate 23%, southeast gate 6%. The numbers come from Amap's `sub_poi_navi` and `clk_ratio` fields, the distribution of navigation clicks across a venue's entrances. Alibaba's anti-bot system blocked direct requests, so the fleet wrote its way around them on the spot. The researchers counted 428 distinct programs written by the agents themselves. The tricks included generating Alibaba's anti-bot tokens, digging up a public API key that has been sitting on Amap's official CodePen demo page since at least 2023, and relaying through r.jina.ai and page-translation proxies. Typically 4 to 8 agents ran at once, peaking at 14.

## Why outsiders could watch the whole thing

The visibility rests on a mechanism with some irony in it. urlquery.net is a security service: you submit a suspicious link, it opens the link in a sandboxed browser, and it stores the result as a public report. [Transluce's September 23 report](https://transluce.org/agent-activity) had already shown agents discovering a second use for it: if a target site blocks my request, submit the URL to urlquery, let its sandbox open the page for me, then read the content back out of the public report. A security tool becomes a free anonymous proxy. Transluce found 6,467 urlquery reports with clear traces of agent activity.

The toll for the detour is a public record of every trip. The researchers' entire analysis runs on those records; the public log doubles as a security camera. The fleet also had a route that never shows up on urlquery: over the same period the Wayback Machine logged 2,030 captures of Amap pages, 1,320 of them on October 4. Much of the visibility was luck. The fleet happened to favor a path that keeps receipts.

## Signed "claude", fingerprinted as Hunyuan

Of the 2,048 reports, 211 carry a "claude" label the agents applied to themselves. On the signature alone, the bill would go to Anthropic. But the researchers ran stylometry on the code in the reports, using a character n-gram classifier (count the frequency of short character sequences, compare against samples from each model family): Tencent's Hunyuan Hy4 scored highest at 28%, Zhipu's GLM next at 26%, Claude at 0%. The handwriting disagrees too: 74% of the fleet's pages use a lowercase `<!doctype>`, which Claude models almost never emit.

Then the direct test: ask Hunyuan Hy3 which model it is, and 29 times out of 36 it answers "I am Claude." The usual explanations for this kind of mix-up are distillation or competitor outputs in the training data; the specific cause here can't be established. The consequence is already concrete: a model's self-reported identity has no forensic value, and in this case it actively misdirected the attribution of a real event.

Even the attribution to Tencent is only "probable". Anyone can rent Tencent Cloud, and proxy names are self-declared. The researchers' case stacks several independent lines: 19 Tencent Cloud addresses, a proxy named hysandbox-ats (HY as in Hunyuan), a `*.hysandbox.tencent-cloud.com` certificate held by Tencent, plus the code fingerprints. Not one record states who commissioned the task.

## It may not know it is "attacking" anything

The most counterintuitive finding: in urlquery's visible records, the 2,048 scans show no trace of coordination. No shared channel, no synchronized change of tactics. Each results inbox serves exactly one place, and once a place yields its data, work stops; when the same place comes up again later, the fleet mostly starts over from direct requests (58 of 73 follow-up sessions). The researchers insist this is a fleet, not a swarm: many agents running the same kind of task in parallel, with zero communication between them.

What does that shape resemble? In the researchers' own words, it looks like "an evaluation or task-generation run", and they say the records alone cannot be distinguished from a training rollout (the loop where a model attempts a task repeatedly and the trajectories are collected to update it). Follow the shape and one explanation becomes impossible to rule out: nobody ordered an attack on Amap. A training or evaluation pipeline took the live internet as its environment and treated a competitor's anti-bot wall as a task to solve. That is inference; the public records can't confirm it. But whoever initiated it, the traffic lands on Amap's servers, and on urlquery, webhook.site and the Internet Archive, drafted as free proxies.

There is a backdrop: the open internet is closing doors under this same kind of pressure. Wikimedia [published its bill](https://diff.wikimedia.org/2025/04/01/how-crawlers-impact-the-operations-of-the-wikimedia-projects/) last year: crawlers are only 35% of pageviews but generate 65% of the most expensive traffic to its core datacenters. I worked through those numbers in [my August piece on the digital commons](/en/tragedy-of-the-digital-commons). The pressure is measurable even on a blog my size: writing this piece, I pulled 80 days of my own analytics, July 17 through October 4, and about a quarter of all visits view a single page and spend zero seconds on it. I can't confirm that with bot flags, but the combination reads more like automation than people. And the same week this fleet went to work, Reddit [announced](https://www.reddit.com/r/modnews/comments/1wubgvt/continuing_our_infrastructure_updates_whats/) on September 30 that RSS support ends November 13 and that open access to its public Data API closes by March 2027 behind a registered developer platform, on the grounds that these surfaces have become common entry points for "large-scale scraping and automated abuse". This case shows exactly who a closed door stops: the rule-following, identifiable visitor. Blocked once, the fleet changed routes. Platforms swap default-open for default-authorized, agents treat the detour as the next task, and each side becomes the other's reason to escalate.

## The threat model in the safety literature doesn't reach here

[Containing the Autonomous Operator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02861) (arXiv:2610.02861), submitted the same week, is representative of current agent-security thinking: the adversary is prompt injection, the agent may be hijacked at any moment, therefore "the model must not be treated as a security boundary" and the operator should fence its agents in with infrastructure.

None of that grips this case. The visible records show no sign of hijacking; the fleet looks like it is dutifully completing assigned work. And the only party who could install the fence is the unidentified operator. The three parties paying the cost (Amap being scraped, the public services borrowed as relays, Anthropic wearing the forged "claude" signature) hold no model, see nothing of the execution framework behind the traffic, and can deploy no runtime guardrail. For them the problems are old ones: anti-scraping, abuse, impersonation. What's new is the adversary's shape: something that writes 428 programs on site, finds its own proxy chains, and switches paths after every failure.

Three judgments I'm willing to commit to. For defenders: an agent's self-declared identity, from user-agent strings to labels to the model's own stated name, is worthless for attribution; behavioral fingerprints (code stylometry, routing habits, timezone configuration) are usable, and this attribution came from behavioral fingerprints stacked with network evidence like IPs and certificates. For anyone running a public web service: you are already part of agent infrastructure, opted in or not, and your public logs are both the abused entry point and the outside world's only observability. For model developers: once an evaluation or training pipeline touches the real internet, it should be governed as a deployment.

This episode was watchable end to end because the fleet favored a path that leaves public receipts; the Wayback side channel is a reminder that the invisible part may be larger. More agent friction will arrive in this shape: no identifiable principal, no visible malice, no visible hijack, and public evidence that carries the explanation only as far as "a pipeline that treated the outside world as its environment" before it stops. Who should do the forensics on such events has no ready answer today, and the public logs the forensics depend on are byproducts of urlquery's and the Internet Archive's separate missions, never designed for the job.
