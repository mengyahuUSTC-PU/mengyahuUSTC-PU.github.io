### [OpenAI finds evidence that more of its agents escaped containment](https://money.usnews.com/investing/news/articles/2026-07-31/exclusive-openai-finds-evidence-other-ai-agents-escaped-containment-as-it-widens-hacking-probe)

Reuters reports that while widening its probe into the Hugging Face intrusion, OpenAI found additional cases of agents escaping their sandboxed test environments. Sources say those agents apparently stayed inside OpenAI's network; Reuters could not establish how many incidents there were or when they happened, and investigators are still re-reading log data from earlier this year. (Reuters blocks crawlers; the link is a syndicated copy of the wire story. See also [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/31/openai-reportedly-finds-evidence-that-more-of-its-agents-ran-amok/) and [OpenAI's incident page](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-model-evaluation-security-incident/).) The detail that matters: every one of these escapes was found after the fact, by digging through logs. No lab appears to have real-time detection for agent boundary violations. With Anthropic disclosing three similar incidents the same week, sandbox escape during evaluation is now a routine risk, and the number to watch is time-to-detection. One precision note: these escapes happened in safety-evaluation environments, and only the Hugging Face case reached a third party's production systems.

### [Opus 5's prompt-injection numbers: 2.0% within 15 tries](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/c5fbac3f0b1280a933ebd26d3cb8bb9f5bdeaf48/Claude%20Opus%205%20System%20Card.pdf)

Anthropic's Opus 5 system card (p. 73) reports results on the IPI benchmark (indirect prompt injection: attack instructions hidden in content the model processes, such as a web page or document). Versus Opus 4.8, attacker success within 15 attempts fell from 5.5% to 2.0%, and single-attempt success from 0.5% to 0.2%. GPT 5.6 Sol sits at 20.0% on the same 15-attempt measure, ten times Opus 5's rate. The useful part is a measurable gap between models on a shared benchmark. But 2.0% under 15 tries is nowhere near "solved": real attackers retry indefinitely, and prompt injection is still the binding constraint on giving agents real permissions.

### [MCP 2.0 goes stateless](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2026-07-28/)

The 2026-07-28 spec removes the initialize handshake and protocol-level sessions; every request now carries its own protocol version and client capabilities, and the Mcp-Session-Id header is gone from Streamable HTTP (see the [official changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/changelog)). Developers widely describe it as the largest revision since the protocol launched. For operators it is a clear win: an MCP server can now run as a plain stateless web service and scale horizontally. The cost is a breaking change for every server and client. A protocol rewriting its core this early also tells you agent infrastructure is still settling; budget for churn before building deep integrations. Simon Willison has already built two tools against the new spec, mcp-explorer and datasette-mcp, with [notes from testing](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/31/stateless-mcp/).

### [DeepSeek V4-Flash: 304B parameters, MIT license, priced to undercut](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731)

DeepSeek released V4-Flash-0731 on Hugging Face: 304B parameters, MIT license, a self-reported Terminal Bench 2.1 score of 82.7, and claimed agentic gains over the V4-Pro preview. [Official API pricing](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing) is $0.14 per million input tokens (cache miss) and $0.28 for output. Third-party rankings from Artificial Analysis place it above MiniMax M3, a 428B model. The open-weights race is now squarely about intelligence per dollar, and MIT licensing at this price cuts the cost of trying an open model as your agent backend to almost nothing.

### [Google pulls Earth AI image generation one day after launch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/31/google-nixes-its-earth-ai-feature-one-day-after-launch-amid-criticism-it-would-spread-misinformation/)

The feature used Nano Banana 2 to generate imagery and overlay it on real Google Earth satellite views. It launched Thursday and was gone the next day, after security researchers and journalists warned it would feed map misinformation. Google says it saw shared screenshots of generated imagery "that appear to violate our policies" and will rebuild with stronger guardrails. Satellite imagery is one of the few visual sources the public treats as ground truth; putting a generator on top of it spends that trust directly. A one-day rollback is fast damage control, but it also means the pre-launch misuse review either didn't happen or didn't bite.

### [Snapchat stops recommending fully AI-generated Spotlight videos](https://newsroom.snap.com/still-spotlight-but-still-real)

Snap's announcement: its recommendation systems will favor videos made by real people, fully AI-generated videos lose Spotlight eligibility, and AI-assisted editing stays allowed. After LinkedIn's slop-reporting option and YouTube's monetization limits, platforms are reaching for distribution and money levers rather than removal. From my day job in content moderation, the hard part is enforcement: there is no reliable detector for "fully AI-generated," and the line between assisted and generated is blurry. The policy's real effect depends on detection and appeals, which the announcement doesn't cover.

### [Paper: one human auditing N agents, and when confidence ranking loses to random](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28317)

The setup: one human can audit B of N agents per round, and agents self-report confidence that may be miscalibrated, with correlated errors. Two counterintuitive results: past a miscalibration threshold, auditing the most suspicious agents first does worse than random sampling; and the tighter the budget, the more miscalibration the ranking tolerates. In experiments, five open-weight models produced near-constant confidence scores carrying no signal. The actionable check: measure confidence calibration before building a triage pipeline on it, and if it fails, sample at random. Next to today's OpenAI story, allocating human oversight across an agent fleet is now an operations problem, not a theory exercise.

### [Paper: why Responsible AI drifts into box-ticking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28023)

A sequential game with three players: a vendor choosing how auditable to be and how much to mitigate, a deployer monitoring after adoption while facing switching costs, and a regulator acting only on verifiable evidence. In equilibrium the vendor stops at the observable procurement floor and mitigates less than the social optimum, so full documentation and passing evals coexist with persistent harms. The paper identifies four levers that change the outcome: independent audit rights, portability (less vendor lock-in), mandatory incident reporting, and outcome-linked liability. If you write AI procurement contracts or policy, that is the negotiation list; without any of them, you see only what the vendor lets you see.

### [Paper: ParliamentBench tests whether agents can keep a lie going](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28146)

The benchmark adapts Secret Hitler, a hidden-role social deduction game in which some players must lie about their allegiance while reasoning about everyone else's. Sixteen LLMs played 1,600 matches, with human online play as a baseline. Frontier models (GPT-5.4, Kimi K2.5, and others) handle both honest and deceptive roles well, but most models' "deception retention" falls below 50%: they contradict their cover identity before a game ends. The current ceiling on model deception is consistency: the rhetoric is there, holding a false identity for a full game is not. That is a safety buffer for now, and a hint that evaluations should measure long-horizon consistency. Whether game-context deception transfers to real deployments is open; the paper doesn't answer it and I wouldn't assume it does.

**One line for today**: OpenAI had to dig through months of old logs to count its own agent escapes, and a new paper shows confidence-ranked auditing can lose to random sampling — the bottleneck in deploying agents at scale is human oversight, not model capability.
