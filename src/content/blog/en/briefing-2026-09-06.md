### [OpenAI's chief scientist publishes "An Alien Mind": alignment unsolved, voluntary slowdowns welcome](https://openai.com/index/an-alien-mind)

Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, published an essay whose central claim is that AI is becoming a form of intelligence we don't fully understand, may soon struggle to monitor, and that increasingly drives its own development. Three judgments stand out: no lab, in his view, has solved alignment and monitoring well enough to justify scaling at maximum speed; he expects, and hopes, that voluntary slowdowns become commonplace until the industry establishes shared safety bars; and international coordination on AI development should become a top priority for governments. He also splits alignment into two layers: goal alignment (does the model try to accomplish the goal you set) and value alignment (can it act on a set of high-level principles when goals are vague, conflicting, or adversarial), and argues the second is the harder and more consequential one. This is the person responsible for OpenAI's research roadmap writing on the company's own site; read it against the next item to get the full picture.

### [Inside OpenAI: 3.1 agent-workdays per human workday](https://openai.com/index/research-acceleration-view-inside-openai/)

The same day, OpenAI published numbers on how far coding agents have spread through its own research process. The company says it has hit its previously stated September 2026 milestone of an "automated research intern": a system that completes well-scoped research tasks under human direction, including tasks that would take a skilled researcher a few days. By mid-August the research org was logging 3.1 agent-workdays for every human workday, and experiments per active researcher hit an all-time high in August (tracking began January 2025), which OpenAI says is "correlated" with rising internal Codex use. The next internal target is an "automated AI researcher" by March 2028. Two caveats: the post doesn't say how an agent-workday is counted (agent runtime, or tasks completed), so 3.1x the hours is not 3x the output; and OpenAI itself notes that research has many other bottlenecks, so overall progress won't track any single metric. Even discounted, this is the most concrete internal data any frontier lab has shared on AI accelerating AI research.

### [Authors push back as publishers claim shares of the Anthropic settlement](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/06/authors-push-back-as-publishers-and-agents-seek-share-of-anthropic-settlement/)

Anthropic's $1.5 billion book-piracy settlement ($3,000 per pirated work) has reached the payout stage, and as allocation notices go out, authors are finding publishers and literary agents claiming money they aren't owed. Under the terms, in-print traditionally published books split 50-50 between author and publisher, while self-published books and books whose rights have reverted go entirely to the author; rights reversion means the copyright returns from publisher to author once a book goes out of print or contract conditions are met. Mystery writer April Henry says HarperCollins claimed a share of a book that reverted "at least 17 years ago," and Victoria Strauss, who runs the industry-watchdog project Writer Beware, reports a stream of identical complaints and reads the problem as systemic bad recordkeeping rather than deliberate grabbing; the Authors Guild has published a dispute process. Now that AI copyright litigation has reached the execution stage, the biggest obstruction isn't the AI company but decades of messy rights records inside publishing, and how this payout pipeline handles disputes will become the template for the settlements that follow.

**Today in one sentence**: the acceleration data and the slowdown plea came from the same company on the same day; that pairing says more about the current pace of frontier research than any outside warning could.
