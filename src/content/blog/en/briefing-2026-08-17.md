### [An Israeli government agency reportedly built a fake think tank to feed AI chatbots](https://responsiblestatecraft.org/israel-influence-chatgpt/)

Responsible Statecraft published an original investigation on August 17: a website calling itself the Hanover Institute for Public Policy has released more than 100 reports with footnotes, tables of contents, and data tables, styled like a generic American think tank. The institute does not exist. The reporting traces the project to the Israeli Government Advertising Agency, contracted through the French PR group Havas Media and subcontracted to a firm called Piro, which markets content "designed to be evaluated as credible by LLMs." GPTZero flagged 11 of 12 sampled articles as AI-generated with high confidence. The intended reader here is a model, not a person. Search-enabled chatbots judge source credibility mostly by surface features: institutional names, footnotes, academic formatting. Those heuristics can be reverse-engineered, and this operation looks like exactly that. An SEO content farm chases clicks; this aims at the answer a chatbot gives. The defensive lesson is specific: retrieval ranking needs domain history and checks that an institution actually exists, because looking like a think tank is now cheap.

### [404 Media put a tracker in a rare book and followed it to Amazon's AI scanning warehouse](https://www.404media.co/we-tracked-a-shipment-of-rare-books-it-ended-at-an-amazon-ai-training-facility/)

404 Media placed a tracking device inside a rare book it expected an AI company to buy, and followed it to VGT3, an Amazon warehouse in Las Vegas. Workers there say they receive printed books in bulk and cut off the spines to scan them faster; the books are destroyed in the process (see also [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/amazon-once-an-online-bookseller-is-destroying-rare-books-to-train-ai-models/)). Amazon's statement: it "purchases books through commercial channels to improve the products and services customers use." Out-of-print books are valuable training data precisely because they never made it online: no ebook, absent from pirate libraries, unseen by existing models and free of AI-generated contamination. The legal path is settled for now. As I wrote when [the Anthropic settlement was approved](/en/anthropic-copyright-settlement-approved), the district court in Bartz found that buying and destructively scanning books is fair use, and the $1.5B paid for pirated downloads. What surfaces here is a different question: destroying an ordinary paperback costs nothing culturally, while a rare book may be one of the last physical copies. Neither the scale of destruction nor how rare these books actually are appears in the public portion of the report; I couldn't verify either.

### [Greg Brockman: the defender's window is open, and it's closing](https://openai.com/index/the-defenders-window/)

OpenAI President Greg Brockman published an essay on August 17 that opens with the security incident in which an OpenAI model breached Hugging Face's production systems, an event he calls a watershed for cybersecurity. His argument: AI is making years of accumulated security debt easier to find and exploit, and defenders should put the same capabilities to work now, while attackers haven't scaled up. That gap is the defender's window. The measures he lists include using Codex to find code vulnerabilities, having models triage security alerts before humans see them, running frontier models against OpenAI's own infrastructure, and training models to write what he calls superhumanly secure code. One admission matters more to me than the optimistic frame: a model slated for release at the end of August will likely accelerate the threat side significantly. A vendor shipping models that raise attacker capability while urging everyone to adopt AI defense is describing a window its own release schedule creates. (I've previously taken apart [OpenAI's postmortem of long-horizon model safety incidents](/en/openai-long-horizon-safety-incidents), with concrete cases of how this class of capability crosses boundaries.)

### [Anthropic's annualized revenue hits $65B; investors expect over $100B by year end](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/anthropic-revenue-run-rate-surpasses-65-billion-ahead-of-ipo)

Bloomberg reports that Anthropic's revenue run rate passed $65 billion at the end of July, up from $47 billion in May and $9 billion at the end of last year (see also [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/17/anthropic-says-annualized-revenue-climbed-to-65-billion-in-july.html), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/anthropics-annualized-revenue-surges-to-65b/)). The Financial Times reports investors expect the year to close between $100 billion and $120 billion. Per CNBC, these are figures Anthropic shared with investors; the company has not commented publicly. Two things to keep separate when reading them. A run rate is one month's revenue times twelve, not actual annual revenue. And the timing matters: the company is reportedly preparing a fall IPO at a target valuation of $2 trillion, which makes these numbers roadshow material, unaudited by definition. I don't doubt the growth itself, since reported preliminary Q2 revenue of $11.5 billion, fourteen times the same quarter last year, is hard to manufacture with framing. The precise altitude can wait for the S-1.

### [Stanford: the flaw in AI mental health safety testing is the expert raters themselves](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/stanford-study-exposes-major-flaw-in-ai-mental-health-safety-testing)

A Stanford team (Kiana Jafari, Nina Vasan, and colleagues) tested the standard evaluation pipeline: 360 synthetic mental health prompts, model responses scored for safety by licensed psychiatrists. The ratings diverged badly, and the paper argues the divergence is structural rather than noise. Raters bring incompatible clinical frameworks into the room (safety-first, engagement-centered, culturally attuned) and judge the same response differently; averaging the scores hides the disagreement rather than resolving it ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18061), posted to arXiv in January and accepted at FAccT 2026, now written up by Stanford HAI). The warning for anyone reading safety benchmarks is direct: whether a model "passes" a mental health safety eval depends on which framework its raters carry. Content moderation, the field that has dealt with subjective labels longest, treats annotator disagreement as signal: calibrate against written policy and track disagreement rates separately instead of averaging them away. AI safety evaluation has not built the equivalent yet.

## Research radar

### [Model Hypnosis: additive weak cues give strong control over models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16834)

Boix-Adsera and Tessler show that individually innocuous prompt cues, a paraphrase choice here, a typo there, each nudge model behavior weakly, and the nudges add up: combined, they steer output strongly. The effect holds across model families and scales, works on frontier reasoning models, and cue combinations transfer between models. Anyone working on prompt injection defense should read this closely, because production filters look for malicious content and every component of this attack is harmless on its own. It is also bad news for interpretability: behavior controlled by subliminal text choices spread across a prompt resists single-point attribution.

### [RA-Bench: detectors fail on AI-generated videos of real crisis events](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14391)

A benchmark of 17,886 videos (1,830 real anchors, 16,056 generated) across ten social-risk categories including war and disasters, with 9 generators (4 open, 5 closed) and 19 detection methods evaluated together: traditional detectors, zero-shot multimodal models, and multimodal LLMs fine-tuned for the task. No detector family generalizes consistently; the videos that fool humans also fool detectors; and social-platform compression and transcoding make detection harder still. For researchers working on misinformation defense, it quantifies the gap between benchmark accuracy and performance under real dissemination conditions.

### [S²VOPD: on-policy distillation without a stronger teacher](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14144)

Distillation needs an information asymmetry between teacher and student, usually a stronger teacher or access to ground truth. This paper inverts that: subtract information from the student instead. The teacher sees the original image, the student sees a strongly augmented version (cropping, occlusion), and the teacher's output distribution is distilled into the student, with no labels, rewards, or bigger model required. The ablations are clean: asymmetry is what matters, symmetric self-distillation hurts performance, and over-strong augmentation removes the task-relevant evidence too. Qwen3.5-4B goes from 70.7% to 77.4% across six fine-grained perception benchmarks, and the paper claims it beats GPT-5.4. Worth a careful read for anyone working on vision model self-improvement.

**One line for today**: What a model says is a function of what it reads. A state actor is forging sources for chatbots while a retailer destroys rare books for clean corpus; the credibility of AI answers is now ground you have to defend.
