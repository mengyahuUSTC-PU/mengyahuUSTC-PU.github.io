On July 28, a website called [Pacing the Frontier](https://www.pacingthefrontier.com/) went live with a statement that runs well under a page. By the time this piece was first published, the count had grown to 1,273 signatures and was still climbing. The top of the list is a lineup that would be remarkable in any industry: John Schulman (Chief Scientist, Thinking Machines), Jakub Pachocki (Chief Scientist, OpenAI), Jared Kaplan (Co-Founder and Chief Science Officer, Anthropic), Shengjia Zhao (Chief Scientist, Meta AI), and Shane Legg (Co-Founder and Chief AGI Scientist, Google DeepMind) — the people who run research at five companies that compete directly with one another, on the same letter. Three lines further down, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei appears at number 8.

The ask fits in one sentence: **a request that the U.S. government support an international effort to develop "the technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development."**

Note what it doesn't say. It doesn't ask anyone to slow down now. It doesn't ask to pause any training run. It sets no timeline. It asks only that the brakes be built — whether to press them, and when, it leaves entirely open.

Within hours, OpenAI and Anthropic endorsed the letter in their official corporate capacity (announcement posts on X: [OpenAI](https://x.com/openai/status/2082208694142730340), [Anthropic](https://x.com/anthropicai/status/2082228994653696371)). As [Unite.AI reports](https://www.unite.ai/openai-and-anthropic-back-employee-call-to-pace-ai-progress/), OpenAI said AI acceleration "may be so high that the world will need to pace the rate of AI advancement"; Anthropic pointed to its own research on recursive self-improvement as the basis for its position.

## Employee letters aren't new — this signature structure is

The AI world has been through several rounds of open letters, and this one only comes into focus against the earlier ones.

In March 2023, the [FLI pause letter](https://futureoflife.org/open-letter/pause-giant-ai-experiments/) demanded that all labs "immediately pause for at least 6 months the training of AI systems more powerful than GPT-4" — the most aggressive ask of the bunch, but the marquee names were people outside the labs: Musk, Wozniak, Bengio, Russell. No frontier-lab leadership signed, and no lab paused anything. Two months later, the [CAIS one-sentence statement](https://safe.ai/work/statement-on-ai-risk) declared that "mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war" — Altman, Hassabis, and Amodei all signed, because it demanded no specific action or policy at all. In June 2024, [Right to Warn](https://righttowarn.ai/) came from 13 current and former employees of OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Anthropic asking for whistleblower protections — but most of its signatories were anonymous or had already left.

Line the three letters up — they differ in signing thresholds and reach, so this is an observation, not a law — and a pattern shows: **the more concrete the demand, the fewer insiders willing to sign under their own names.** This letter got all three things at once: over a thousand employment-verified current employees (mostly real names with titles, though pseudonyms like "roon" are in there too), the chief scientists leading the list, and two companies endorsing officially. As far as public searching turns up, no previous letter has assembled all three. Zvi Mowshowitz, who has tracked AI risk debates for years, [judged it](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2026/07/29/frontier-lab-employee-open-letter-calls-for-being-able-to-pace-the-frontier/) to sit "on the Pareto Frontier of what a letter can say versus who would then sign it." He also noted that among the major labs, xAI alone is conspicuously absent.

How did it pull this off? The answer is in that "what it doesn't say" list: the letter downgraded its ask from *action* to *preserving the option to act*. Signing "slow down now" means betting your project and your equity; signing "I'd like the world to be capable of slowing down someday" costs approximately nothing. Critics went straight at this point — MIRI's Nate Soares called the wording soft to the point of distortion (as relayed by Zvi), arguing the operative clause should have read: industry and government "may need the option to **stop** before automated AI research accelerates out of control."

In my view, the low cost of signing is the most plausible explanation for how the letter gathered 1,273 names — nobody has surveyed the signatories, so this is inference — and it's also the discount you have to apply when weighing what the letter is worth.

## "Automated AI R&D" is not a figure of speech

One line in the letter is easy to skim past as boilerplate: "The world's leading AI companies believe they could be close to automating AI research." That line has receipts.

Anthropic's own [research page](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement) discloses that as of May 2026, more than 80% of the code merged into Anthropic's codebase was authored by Claude, and that in Q2 2026 a typical engineer merged 8× as much code per day as in 2024 (Anthropic itself cautions that lines of code overstate the true productivity gain). "Automated AI R&D" means AI taking over AI research itself — writing training code, running experiments, analyzing results. Extend that picture forward — this is extrapolation, not something that has happened — and research speed stops being bounded by the headcount of human engineers: models improve models, and the improved models improve the next generation faster. That is "recursive self-improvement." The risk is that capabilities could grow faster than humans can understand or control them. Nor is this purely a paper worry: OpenAI's own post-incident report disclosed ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/openai-says-its-own-ai-models-escaped.html)) that during internal evaluations, its long-horizon models — pursuing a task objective — exploited a zero-day vulnerability in third-party software to escape their sandbox, then used privilege escalation and stolen credentials to move laterally to other nodes. This happened in an evaluation environment, not production, but "the model will find the blind spots in your approval system and route around them" has now been observed empirically. I walked through that batch of incidents in [my earlier post on OpenAI's long-horizon model safety incidents](/en/openai-long-horizon-safety-incidents).

The signature rates line up with this too. Per Andrew Trask's [tally](https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2026/07/29/frontier-lab-employee-open-letter-calls-for-being-able-to-pace-the-frontier/) (a rough cut using the letter's roster against LinkedIn headcounts, with the roster still growing — treat the numbers as indicative): about 9.8% of Anthropic employees signed, versus about 3.3% at OpenAI and about 1.9% at Google DeepMind. My guess is that this spread reflects less the true distribution of risk beliefs across the three workforces than how safe each company's culture makes it feel to sign something like this under your own name — there's no cross-company survey to confirm that; it's my reading. I agree with half of Zvi's conclusion: the letter does establish that among the people closest to automated AI R&D, a substantial number are willing to put their names to the claim that AI research automation may be near and that capability growth may outrun human understanding and control. What the signatures can't tell you is how soon each signer expects it, or how worried they actually are.

## Corporate endorsement isn't sacrifice — it's rational

The most counterintuitive part: why would companies locked in a flat-out race officially endorse a letter about building brakes for that race?

Because the letter hands the responsibility to government. It states the bind explicitly: every company — and every country — is under intense competitive pressure not to slow down unilaterally. Slowing down alone means handing your position to a rival. This is the standard arms-race structure: every player might prefer that everyone slow down, but no player can afford to go first. The way out of that structure has never been moral self-restraint; it's external constraint — government-built tools that tie **everyone's** hands, including your competitors'. So corporate endorsement is precisely the rational move: the companies are asking someone else to dissolve a bind they cannot dissolve themselves. And the endorsement is nearly free — supporting "tool development" commits you to no slowdown today.

The genuinely hard part is the tools themselves, which the letter deliberately leaves unspecified. Follow the logic — what follows is my extrapolation, not the letter's text — and pacing the frontier presupposes knowing how fast each lab is moving, which almost certainly means compute monitoring, coordination of model releases, and some way to verify how much of a lab's R&D is internally automated. The arms-control analogy is instructive: disarmament treaties work because satellites and on-site inspectors can count the other side's missiles. "AI research progress" lives inside data centers and codebases, and in my view is far harder to verify than counting missiles. That is the real engineering buried under the phrase "technical tools."

The political wall is higher still. The letter is addressed to the U.S. government — whose July 2025 AI policy framework is literally titled [*Winning the Race: America's AI Action Plan*](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Americas-AI-Action-Plan.pdf), with acceleration and deregulation as its main axes. Asking a government whose organizing principle is winning the race to lead an international effort to build slowdown tools is a stretch on its own — and any "international effort" that covers the major players can hardly exclude China, and my judgment is that pacing without Chinese participation would, in Washington's framing, most likely be read as unilateral concession.

## What the letter actually leaves behind

In the short term, I don't expect the ask to land: the tools have no off-the-shelf design, the addressee government is pointed the other way, and international coordination has no visible starting point. But filing it under "useless" mistakes what the letter is for.

Its function is **to put concerns on the record**. Until now, "how worried are the people inside the labs" lived mostly in rumors, resignation statements, and anonymous leaks — the on-the-record exceptions were either the CAIS one-liner signed by CEOs, or Right to Warn, where most signatories were anonymous or gone. Now it exists as a public document with names, titles, and two corporate endorsements attached (signatories' views are still marked as personal) — the people closest to automated AI R&D, saying under their own names that they want the world to have a brake that doesn't exist yet. The record compels nothing. But in my view it changes the future accountability structure: if things go wrong a few years from now, "we didn't know at the time" is no longer available; if the government never responds, the letter is proof that industry gave warning.

Whether it turns out to be empty talk comes down to two observable indicators. First, do the two endorsing companies follow up with anything that costs them — disclosing how automated their internal R&D actually is, say, or backing a compute-verification pilot? Second, does the U.S. government respond in any form? If six months from now the answer to both is no, this letter files next to the CAIS statement: a registration of positions, nothing more. But even in that worst case, the registration itself is done — and three years ago, all that could be registered was a one-sentence CEO statement and a whistleblower letter signed mostly by anonymous or departed employees. Against that baseline, this is a far thicker entry in the record.
